# Psychological Triggers

The "why" under direct response. These aren't manipulation tricks — they're the underlying drives that make humans take action. Use them ethically (with real proof, real scarcity, real promises) and your copy converts. Use them dishonestly and you build a list of people who hate you.

---

## The Life-Force 8 (Cashvertising)

Eight biologically programmed desires that drive all human behavior. Every piece of copy should tap **at least one**. The deeper you go on a primary LF8, the stronger the response.

| #  | Desire                              | What it really means in copy                                    |
|----|-------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 1  | **Survival, life extension**        | Living longer, being healthier, avoiding death.                  |
| 2  | **Enjoyment of food and beverages** | Pleasure from eating and drinking.                               |
| 3  | **Freedom from fear, pain, danger** | Security, safety, protection.                                    |
| 4  | **Sexual companionship**            | Attraction, relationships, intimacy.                              |
| 5  | **Comfortable living conditions**   | Physical comfort, luxury, ease.                                   |
| 6  | **Superiority / winning**           | Status, keeping up with peers, being the best.                   |
| 7  | **Care of loved ones**              | Family wellbeing, children's future, protection.                  |
| 8  | **Social approval**                 | Being liked, respected, accepted.                                |

### How to use them

Don't try to hit all 8. Pick **the primary one** for your audience and surface it explicitly. Layer 1–2 secondary ones if natural.

#### Examples by niche

| Niche                                | Primary LF8                        | How to surface it                                       |
|--------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------|
| Fitness for men 30–50                | Sexual companionship + Superiority  | "Get the body you had at 25." / "Stop looking like a tired dad." |
| B2B coaching                         | Superiority + Social approval       | "Stop being the broke coach in the room."               |
| Insurance / financial planning       | Care of loved ones + Freedom from fear | "What happens to your kids if you're not here?"      |
| Premium real estate                  | Comfortable living + Superiority    | "The home that says you've made it."                    |
| Health supplements                   | Survival + Freedom from pain        | "Energy at 50 you didn't have at 30."                   |

These desires are **hardwired**. They beat learned desires (curiosity, novelty, prestige) every time when properly surfaced.

## The six weapons of influence (Cialdini)

The six structural triggers behind most "why people say yes" research. Use them in combination — not alone.

### 1. Reciprocity

> *"When you give first, people feel obligated to give back."*

**In copy:**
- Give a free training, free chapter, free strategy session.
- The gift must be **genuinely valuable** — not a thinly-disguised pitch.
- Lead with: *"Let me share something that took me 5 years to learn..."*

### 2. Commitment & consistency

> *"Once people commit to something small, they want to stay consistent with it."*

**In copy:**
- Get a small "yes" first — a click, a download, a question answered.
- The opt-in is the small commitment. The purchase becomes consistent with that commitment.
- Two-step opt-ins (click "yes" before showing the form) outperform one-step.

### 3. Social proof

> *"People look at what others are doing to decide what's right."*

**In copy:**
- Specific numbers: "847 clients", not "thousands."
- Named clients: "Sarah K., marketing director", not "Anonymous user."
- Photos and videos > text testimonials.
- Third-party validation > self-promotion (TrustPilot, BBB, media mentions).

### 4. Authority

> *"People defer to those they perceive as expert."*

**In copy:**
- State credentials in context: *"After coaching 200+ businesses through 7-figure exits..."*
- Don't brag — let the proof speak.
- Borrow authority through trusted associations (mentioning credible publications, mentors, partners).

### 5. Liking

> *"People say yes to people they like."*

**In copy:**
- Show personality. Share a flaw or struggle.
- Find common ground with the audience early.
- Photos of you (the founder) outperform stock photography by 30–50%.
- Use humor where appropriate. Self-deprecating > sarcastic.

### 6. Scarcity

> *"People want what they can't easily have."*

**In copy:**
- Real scarcity only. Capacity ("12 spots"), time ("doors close Sunday at midnight"), or seasonal.
- Manufactured scarcity ("only 3 left!" every week) burns trust permanently.
- Pair scarcity with reasoning: *"I'm capping at 12 because each client gets 2 calls a week with me personally."*

## The Boron Letters principles (Gary Halbert)

The most concentrated direct-response wisdom ever written. Five principles that should be in every copywriter's bones.

### 1. Sell people what they want to buy

> *"The first rule of marketing is: sell people what they want to buy."*

Don't try to convince people they want what you sell. Find people who already want it.

- Observe what people buy, not what they say they want.
- Look for **starving crowds** — markets with proven, urgent demand.
- The best copy and the best offer can't beat marketing to people with existing desire.

**In practice:** spend more time researching audiences than writing copy. Reddit threads, Amazon reviews, and survey data are where the real angles live.

### 2. The A-Pile vs. B-Pile

> *"People sort communication into two piles. A-Pile gets opened first. B-Pile may never get opened."*

- **A-Pile:** Looks personal. Real envelope. Real handwriting. No teaser copy.
- **B-Pile:** Obviously commercial. Glossy. Teaser copy on the outside. "Free!" stamps.

**Modern application:**
- Email subject lines that look personal ("Hey", "Re: your call", "Quick question") outperform branded subjects.
- Plain-text emails outperform heavily-designed ones.
- Direct-response ads that mimic organic content (UGC style, native creator look) beat polished brand ads on cold traffic.

### 3. The "you are special" approach

> *"I am offering you this deal because you (by virtue of some unique circumstance) are so special."*

Make the recipient feel uniquely qualified for the offer. Specific, identifiable reasons work better than generic flattery.

**In copy:**
- "Because you signed up for [specific lead magnet] in March..."
- "Since you've been on this list for 6+ months and engaged with every email..."
- "I'm only sending this to people who registered for the webinar but didn't attend..."

The more specific the "why you," the higher the response rate.

### 4. The "reason why" mechanism

> *"People believe and respond more when there's a logical explanation."*

Always give a reason for the offer, the price, the urgency, the discount. The reason can be simple — even slightly arbitrary — and it still doubles response.

**In copy:**
- "I'm running this offer because I'm closing the cohort this month."
- "I dropped the price by 30% because I want to test a new ad angle."
- "Limited to 50 because I run every onboarding call myself."

The reason makes the offer feel real, not random. People believe what they can rationalize.

### 5. Specific > general

> *"Specifics build credibility. Generalities destroy it."*

| Vague                          | Specific                                                     |
|--------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| "Most people see results."      | "77.6% of clients hit their first $10k month within 90 days." |
| "Our clients love us."          | "847 clients, 4.9 average rating across 312 verified reviews."|
| "About a month."                | "31 days."                                                    |
| "Lots of testimonials."         | "Read 142 reviews on TrustPilot."                             |
| "Several major clients."        | "Worked with Adidas, Nike, and three NBA teams."             |

Specificity is credibility. Adjectives are noise.

## Scarcity, social proof, authority — the practical playbook

The three most-used triggers in modern direct response. How to deploy each correctly.

### Scarcity

#### Real types of scarcity (use these)

| Type            | What it is                                          | Example                                                    |
|-----------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Capacity**    | You can only handle X clients/customers              | "I cap at 12 because each gets 2 calls a week with me."   |
| **Time**        | A real deadline                                      | "Doors close Sunday at midnight."                          |
| **Cohort**      | A group launches together                            | "Next cohort starts April 1. After that, May 1."           |
| **Inventory**   | Physical product, real stock                         | "We have 84 units left at this price."                     |
| **Seasonal**    | Tied to a real time of year                         | "This bonus expires when Q1 ends."                         |

#### Fake scarcity (don't)

- "Only 3 left!" every week (visible in archives — burns trust the moment it's caught).
- "Doors close in 24 hours!" then doors stay open indefinitely.
- "Limited time!" with no actual end time.

One detection of fake scarcity = full list permanently discounts your future urgency claims.

### Social proof

#### Strongest forms (top to bottom)

1. **Video testimonial with specific numbers and named client.**
2. **Screenshot of client result + first name.**
3. **Specific written testimonial with name, title, photo.**
4. **Aggregate stats** ("847 clients, 4.9 stars across 312 reviews").
5. **Media logos** ("As seen in Forbes, TechCrunch...") — only works if real and recent.
6. **Generic written testimonials with first names only.** (Weakest. Easy to fake. Reader knows.)

**Rule:** never fabricate. One detection = trust collapse.

### Authority

Build authority through:

- **Specific years of experience** in the niche.
- **Specific named clients you've worked with.**
- **Specific outcomes you've produced** (not "helped many people" — "helped 47 coaches scale to $30k+/mo").
- **Borrowed authority** — mentioning credible mentors, partners, or publications you've been featured in.

Avoid:

- Bragging tone ("I'm the best in the industry...").
- Vague claims ("industry-leading expertise...").
- Inflated credentials.

## The Cashvertising 17 (summary)

The 17 principles from *Cashvertising* by Drew Eric Whitman. Use these as a checklist when reviewing copy that's underperforming.

1. **Fear factor** — Real concern + specific believable solution.
2. **Ego morphing** — Help readers see themselves using the product.
3. **Transfer** — Associate with trusted symbols/authorities.
4. **Bandwagon effect** — Show others are doing it.
5. **Means-end chain** — Sell the benefit of the benefit (don't stop at "saves time" — get to "more time with kids").
6. **Awareness stage matching** — Match copy to where they are (`frameworks.md`).
7. **Inoculation theory** — Pre-empt objections before they're raised.
8. **Belief re-ranking** — Change what the reader prioritizes.
9. **Elaboration likelihood** — Some readers want detail (central). Others want shortcuts (peripheral). Provide both.
10. **Six weapons of influence** — Cialdini (above).
11. **Message organization** — Structure matters as much as content.
12. **Examples > statistics** — Stories beat data for persuasion. (Use both. Stories first.)
13. **Message sidedness** — Address counterarguments to build credibility.
14. **Repetition** — Repeat key messages for retention.
15. **Rhetorical questions** — Engage readers mentally without demanding answers.
16. **Evidence** — Proof builds belief. Add proof everywhere.
17. **Heuristics** — Use mental shortcuts (price = quality, popularity = quality).

## The "create mental pictures" principle

The single most underused technique in modern copy.

> *"Use vivid imagery that helps the reader experience the benefit."*

Bad copy describes benefits abstractly:

> *"You'll have more energy and feel better."*

Good copy paints a sensory scene:

> *"You wake up at 6am without an alarm. You're already half-dressed for the gym before you remember it's a workout day. By 9am you've trained, eaten, knocked out the hardest task on your list — and the day hasn't really started yet. That's what 'more energy' actually feels like."*

**How to do it:**

- Describe sensory details (sight, sound, touch, smell, taste).
- Walk the reader through a scene minute by minute.
- Use "you" as the actor in the scene.
- Make abstract benefits ("more energy", "better focus") concrete and tangible.

**Where it works:**

- Sales page hero sections.
- VSL transformation reveals.
- Email "imagine if..." passages.
- Ad scripts (especially video).

## Triggers checklist — every piece of copy should have

Before delivery, confirm:

- [ ] At least one **Life-Force 8 desire** surfaced explicitly.
- [ ] At least one form of **social proof** (specific numbers or named client).
- [ ] **Authority** established through context, not bragging.
- [ ] If scarcity is used, it's **real** (capacity, time, cohort, inventory, or seasonal).
- [ ] At least one **mental picture** painted in the body.
- [ ] **Reciprocity** — reader gets something valuable before being asked to take action.
- [ ] **Inoculation** — top 2–3 objections addressed before they're raised.
- [ ] **Specific over vague** — every claim has a number, name, or concrete detail.
